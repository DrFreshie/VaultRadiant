# Student manuscript — South Africa

*Model answer for the oral English B exam. Approximately eight minutes at a measured pace, with short pauses. Headings are speaking cues, not part of the spoken presentation. Connections are to Mandela’s “I Am Prepared to Die” and the film Invictus from the South Africa unit.*

## Introduction

I’m going to talk about Oren Gruenbaum’s article, “‘Unlock the door or we’ll kick it down’: why South Africa’s youngest politician is in a hurry for change.” It was published in The Guardian on 24 May 2024, shortly before the South African election.

First, I’ll briefly introduce the article. Then I’ll focus on the characterisation of Fasiha Hassan and the intention of the text. Finally, I’ll connect it to Mandela’s speech and the film Invictus.

My main point is that the article shows a difference between having political freedom and actually having equal opportunities.

## Brief introduction to the text

The text is a journalistic profile of Hassan, a young ANC politician. She became known through the FeesMustFall student protests and wants to change the party from within.

The opening places her in a wider context. The so-called “born-free” generation grew up without apartheid, but many are disappointed with the ANC. They don’t necessarily support it just because it helped liberate the country. The article mentions corruption, inequality and power cuts as reasons for this disappointment.

So the central conflict is between the ANC’s proud history and its problems in the present. Hassan belongs to the party, but she also challenges it.

## Characterisation of Fasiha Hassan

Hassan is presented as ambitious, outspoken and impatient. The journalist points out that she is thirty, the same age as democratic South Africa. This makes her seem like a representative of a whole generation, not just an individual politician.

There is both direct and indirect characterisation. The journalist directly describes her as being “in a hurry.” We also learn about her through her actions and quotations. Her involvement in student protests suggests that she is willing to challenge authority.

A central quotation is: “Unlock the door or we kick it down.” The door is a metaphor for access to political power. Older politicians are effectively keeping younger people outside. Hassan demands that they let her generation in.

The image sounds aggressive, but in this context I understand it as political pressure rather than a literal threat. It makes her appear determined and gives the article a dramatic headline. Her use of “we” also shows that she speaks for a group.

However, she isn’t presented as someone who rejects all older people. She says that the party needs an “intergenerational mix.” That suggests she wants cooperation, but not a situation where the older generation controls everything.

The article also shows her vulnerability. As a young Muslim woman, she faces criticism about her clothes and behaviour. People sometimes assume that she is a secretary rather than a politician. These examples show that formal political rights don’t automatically mean equal treatment.

I think the quotation about her car is especially important. She says: “I’m afraid to go to my car late at night, but I have a car. It’s a huge privilege.” She recognises that she experiences insecurity, but also that other women have fewer resources. This makes her appear self-aware and helps build her ethos, or credibility.

There is also a tension in her characterisation. She criticises the ANC’s corruption but still asks people to believe that the party can change. The text doesn’t prove that she can solve this problem. It mainly shows her determination to try.

## Intention and presentation

The article’s intention is partly to inform readers about South African politics before the election. But by focusing on Hassan, it also gives these political issues a human face.

For an international audience, her experiences make large problems like inequality and corruption easier to understand. The short explanations of the ANC’s situation also help readers who don’t know much about South Africa.

The tone towards Hassan is mainly sympathetic. We hear her voice through many direct quotations, while we don’t hear opposing voices from older ANC politicians or her critics. This gives us access to her perspective, but it also means that her claims aren’t strongly challenged in the excerpt.

For example, the detail about her being mistaken for a secretary encourages sympathy. Her education and political experience establish credibility. Together, these details make readers more likely to take her seriously.

I wouldn’t say that the article simply tells readers to support the ANC. It includes serious criticism of the party. Instead, it seems to ask whether people like Hassan can renew it. The article combines hope about a younger generation with uncertainty about whether change is possible.

## Connections to the South Africa unit

This connects to Nelson Mandela’s “I Am Prepared to Die,” which we studied. In that speech, Mandela describes the injustice of apartheid and argues for a democratic society with equal opportunities.

Hassan’s generation has inherited the political freedom that Mandela fought for. But she says: “We’ve not reached a point of economic freedom.” For me, that is the most important contrast in the article. Winning the right to vote was essential, but it didn’t automatically remove poverty or unequal opportunities.

Mandela also discusses poverty and education in his speech, so Hassan’s concerns aren’t completely new. The difference is that Mandela was fighting an apartheid government, while Hassan is challenging the failures of a democratic government led by his own party.

I would also connect the article to Invictus. The film presents Mandela trying to unite a divided country through rugby. It emphasises reconciliation and the hope of building a shared national identity.

The article gives a less optimistic picture of what followed. Hassan says: “We haven’t healed.” She even suggests a Truth and Reconciliation Commission dealing with gender violence in communities. This shows that reconciliation is not just something that happened after apartheid. It is still unfinished work.

## Conclusion

To conclude, Hassan is characterised as a determined but also vulnerable politician who wants change from inside the ANC. The article mainly presents her sympathetically, while leaving open whether her project can succeed.

Seen in relation to our South Africa unit, the text shows that the struggle for equality did not end with apartheid. The challenge for Hassan’s generation is to turn political freedom into real improvements in everyday life.
