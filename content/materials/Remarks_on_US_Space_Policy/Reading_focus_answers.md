# Remarks on US Space Policy — reading notes

George W. Bush, 14 January 2004 · [Speech](Remarks_on_US_Space_Policy.pdf)

**References use page + line numbers**, because line numbering restarts on each page. These notes concern Bush’s proposals, not what actually happened afterwards.

## 1. Rhetorical devices

| Device | Example and reference | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Repetition / anaphora | “We will … We will …” (p. 1, ll. 13–15) | Makes the plan sound confident and determined. |
| Inclusive pronouns | “we”, “our”, “us” throughout | Creates unity between the president, NASA and the public. |
| Tricolon / parallelism | “one mission, one voyage, one landing at a time” (p. 2, ll. 32–33) | Gives the ambitious project a manageable, memorable rhythm. |
| Historical analogy | Lewis and Clark’s expedition compared with space exploration (p. 1, ll. 18–25) | Presents space travel as a continuation of American exploration. |
| Extended journey metaphor | “set a new course”, “new ships”, “new foothold” (p. 1, ll. 12–16) | Frames space policy as a shared voyage into a new frontier. |
| Metaphor | “human thirst for knowledge” (p. 4, ll. 10–12) | Makes curiosity seem a fundamental human need. |
| Antithesis | “a journey, not a race” (p. 5, ll. 1–2) | Emphasises cooperation rather than rivalry. |
| Ethos | Praise for NASA’s values (p. 1, ll. 1–10); Cernan quotation (p. 3, ll. 27–31) | Builds credibility through expertise and respected astronauts. |
|: Logos | Practical technologies (p. 2, ll. 1–9); lunar cost savings (p. 3, ll. 32–40) | Justifies exploration through useful results and economic reasoning. |
| Pathos | Columbia’s dead and “our children and yours” (p. 5, ll. 13–20) | Appeals to grief, courage and responsibility to future generations. |

## 2. American history, values and humanity

- **National character:** “daring, discipline, ingenuity, and unity” (p. 1, ll. 5–6); exploration and understanding are “part of our character” (p. 1, ll. 23–25).
- **Westward expansion:** Lewis and Clark and the Louisiana Purchase (p. 1, ll. 18–22). Space becomes a new frontier. This echoes Manifest Destiny and the frontier tradition, although neither is explicitly named.
- **Pioneering spirit:** “trailblazers” (p. 4, l. 7) and a new generation of “innovators and pioneers” (p. 4, ll. 24–26).
- **Space history:** Shuttle achievements (p. 2, ll. 10–15), Apollo (p. 3, ll. 19–20), Cernan’s last lunar visit (p. 3, ll. 27–31), and Columbia’s loss (p. 5, ll. 13–20).
- **Patriotism and faith:** exploration “lifts our national spirit”; “May God bless” (p. 5, ll. 21–25).
- **Universal benefits:** advances benefit “all of humanity” (p. 1, ll. 7–10); Cernan’s “peace and hope for all mankind” (p. 3, ll. 29–30); curiosity is shared by human beings (p. 4, ll. 10–18; p. 5, ll. 21–24).
- **International cooperation:** obligations to 15 ISS partners (p. 2, ll. 34–36); invitation to other nations in “cooperation and friendship” (p. 4, ll. 39–40–p. 5, ll. 1–2); remembrance of an allied astronaut (p. 5, ll. 15–17).

## 3. Journeys, knowledge and progress

Grouped references covering the speech’s recurring motifs:

| Passage | Main references |
|---|---|
| p. 1, ll. 7–16 | Expanded knowledge, technological advances, a new course, ships, the moon and journeys beyond. |
| p. 1, ll. 18–25–p. 2, ll. 1–9 | Lewis and Clark’s journey; discovery, mapping and understanding; practical progress in communications, computing, medicine and navigation. |
| p. 2, ll. 10–29 | Shuttle research, ISS construction, telescopes, probes, water and life searches; past successes contrasted with stalled human exploration. |
| p. 2, ll. 30–41–p. 3, ll. 1–5 | Extending human presence; progress through individual voyages; learning how humans can survive long journeys. |
| p. 3, ll. 6–31 | Shuttle return, new spacecraft, travel beyond orbit, robotic preparation and a human return to the moon. |
| p. 3, ll. 32–41–p. 4, ll. 1–3 | Lunar resources and technology testing as stepping stones to cheaper, more ambitious exploration. |
| p. 4, ll. 4–26 | Knowledge gained on the moon enables Mars missions; robotic trailblazers supply data; human curiosity, distant travel, breakthroughs and scientific education. |
| p. 4, ll. 39–40–p. 5, ll. 1–12 | International discovery as a shared journey; future funding linked to progress. |
| p. 5, ll. 13–24 | Risk and sacrifice; exploring space compared with crossing unknown lands and seas; “continue the journey”. |

**Overall:** Bush links physical journeys with scientific discovery, technological improvement and national purpose.

## 4. Proposed timeline

| Target | Milestone | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| As soon as safely possible | Return the Shuttle to flight to finish the ISS. | p. 3, ll. 6–10 |
| Within four months of its first meeting | Advisory commission reports on implementation. | p. 4, ll. 27–38 |
| By 2008 | Develop and test the Crew Exploration Vehicle. | p. 3, ll. 13–15 |
| No later than 2008 | Begin robotic lunar missions. | p. 3, ll. 21–24 |
| By 2010 | Complete the ISS; retire the Shuttle in 2010. | p. 2, ll. 34–36; p. 3, ll. 10–12 |
| No later than 2014 | First crewed mission of the new vehicle. | p. 3, ll. 13–15 |
| As early as 2015; by 2020 | Begin extended human lunar missions, with a return to the moon by 2020. | p. 3, ll. 21–27 |
| Later; no fixed date | Use lunar experience to enable human missions to Mars and beyond. | p. 4, ll. 4–7 |
