# The Future Is Written

This unit explores how imagined futures illuminate the values, anxieties, and power structures of the present. Science fiction is approached as a way of asking questions about identity, autonomy, memory, grief, and technological progress rather than simply predicting future inventions.

The opening chapter of Aldous Huxley's *Brave New World* introduces utopia and dystopia, world-building, and the organisation of society through science. Students examine how exposition and scientific language establish a fictional world and compare the chapter with the opening of its television adaptation. T. Coraghessan Boyle's *The Relive Box* and the *Black Mirror* episode “Be Right Back” shift the focus to intimate relationships, escapism, and the ethical implications of technologies that promise to recover the past.

Non-fiction material on human–robot relationships supports discussion of emotional attachment and artificial intelligence. George W. Bush's speech on US space policy connects technological optimism to American history and values, revisiting expansion, Manifest Destiny, and the frontier from the previous unit. Students compare fictional warnings with political visions of progress.

## Key terms

*Science fiction, utopia, dystopia, hard and soft science fiction, near and far future, world-building, cognitive estrangement, info-dump, conditioning, autonomy, memory, escapism, artificial intelligence, grief, progress, frontier, Manifest Destiny, rhetorical devices, register.*

## Working methods and skills

- Close reading, vocabulary work, and text-based group discussion.
- Visual world-building supported by quotations from the text.
- Comparison of prose fiction and television adaptation.
- Scene analysis using framing, perspective, sound, and lighting; presentation with film stills.
- Student-generated analytical questions and oral-exam-style speaking notes.
- Writing for different audiences: formal and informal manuals and a formal argumentative email.
- Rhetorical analysis of a political speech, with attention to historical references and American values.
