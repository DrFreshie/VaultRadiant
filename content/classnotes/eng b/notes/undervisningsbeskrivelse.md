## General description

The course develops students' ability to understand, analyse, and discuss English-language texts and to communicate independently in spoken and written English. Three thematic units connect questions of personal identity with cultural representations of America and imagined technological futures. Students work with fiction and non-fiction in written, visual, audio, interactive, and audiovisual forms, attending to the relationship between content, form, audience, and context.

Analytical work progresses from characterisation, setting, narrative perspective, and theme to rhetorical analysis and comparison across genres and media. Students practise distinguishing summary from analysis and interpretation, substantiating claims with textual evidence, and relating individual texts to broader cultural and social questions. American regional identities, expansion, freedom, and the frontier provide a recurring cultural perspective, while British and American science fiction opens discussion of technology, power, and human relationships.

Oral English is practised through pair work, reading circles, structured discussions, read-aloud activities, and short presentations in an oral-exam-style format. Written work includes summaries, analytical paragraphs, discussions, essay introductions and outlines, creative writing, and audience-specific functional texts. Topic sentences and the ACE/PEE model support coherence and the use of evidence. Vocabulary, pronunciation, grammar, and register are developed alongside the thematic work, using materials including *Getting Started* and minlæring.

## Identity

This introductory unit explores how identity is shaped by family, memory, possessions, work, and personal narratives. *What Remains of Edith Finch* provides the central point of reference for examining public and private identities, inheritance, trauma, and escapism. Poetry, photography, short fiction, podcast material, and television broaden the discussion and introduce analysis across media.

Students develop an analytical vocabulary for characterisation, setting, narrative perspective, structure, and theme, including attention to how language, cinematic techniques, and game mechanics create meaning. Creative responses in Twine and PostSecret lead into summaries, evidence-based paragraphs, and written discussion. Grammar focuses on verb phrases, tenses, progressive forms, do-support, modal verbs, phrasal verbs, and verb patterns.

## The American South

This unit compares representations of the American South in journalism, podcast storytelling, fiction, and television. Students examine how stereotypes, dialect, narrative perspective, and historical context shape portrayals of place and people. Gonzo journalism and narrative podcasting introduce questions of subjectivity and the rhetorical situation, while *Huckleberry Finn* develops attention to irony and the difference between narrator and reader.

Whole-work reading of *West* extends the discussion to expansion, the frontier, freedom, gender, and displacement. Reading circles support independent reading, vocabulary work, and text-based discussion. Written work progresses from analytical paragraphs to introductions, essay outlines, and an analytical essay; oral work includes recorded pronunciation practice and exam-style presentations. Grammar covers nouns, subject–verb agreement, and active/passive voice.

### Texts and materials

- Hunter S. Thompson: *The Kentucky Derby Is Decadent and Depraved* — gonzo journalism.
- Brian Reed: *S-Town*, Chapter 1 — narrative podcast.
- Mark Twain: *The Adventures of Huckleberry Finn*, Chapters 1–2 — novel excerpt.
- Carys Davies: *West* — whole novel.
- *Lovecraft Country*, Episode 1 — television.
- Lawrence Wright: *Donkeys* — interview excerpt on narrative engagement.
- *Southern Stories for Y'all*: introduction to Southern American English.
- *Narrating Truth* — teaching material on journalism and non-fiction.

## The Future Is Written

This ongoing unit examines imagined futures as reflections on the present. Students explore utopia and dystopia, world-building, social conditioning, autonomy, memory, grief, and the ethical implications of technological progress. *Brave New World*, *The Relive Box*, and “Be Right Back” connect social visions to intimate relationships and the desire to recover or escape the past.

Comparison of prose and television develops attention to the expressive possibilities of different media. Non-fiction on human–robot relationships supports ethical discussion, while George W. Bush's space-policy speech connects technological optimism to American values, Manifest Destiny, and the frontier. Students practise scene analysis, oral-exam-style presentations, rhetorical analysis, and writing in formal and informal registers for specific audiences.

### Texts and materials

- Aldous Huxley: *Brave New World*, Chapter 1 — novel excerpt.
- *Brave New World*, Season 1, Episode 1 — first 18 minutes of the television adaptation.
- T. Coraghessan Boyle: *The Relive Box* — short story.
- *Black Mirror*: “Be Right Back”, Season 2, Episode 1 — television.
- Louisa Hall: *How We Feel About Robots That Feel* — article.
- George W. Bush: *Remarks on US Space Policy*, 14 January 2004 — speech.
- *The Future Is Written: A Brief Introduction…* — coursebook material on science fiction.
- *Can AI 'cure' grief?* — supplementary online video.
