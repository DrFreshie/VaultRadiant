# Genres as interpretive lenses

A genre is not just a box that a text belongs in. It is an interpretive lens: a way of asking what a text is trying to do and how it usually does it.

In this framework, genres are defined by a combination of:

- **core intent**: what the text is mainly trying to do, such as warn, persuade, entertain, document, criticize, speculate, or reflect
- **core conventions**: the typical methods, structures, themes, characters, settings, or stylistic features the genre often uses

Fiction and nonfiction are treated as a separate dimension. They describe a text's relationship to reality:

- **Fiction** presents imagined events, characters, or situations.
- **Nonfiction** presents itself as engaging with real people, events, arguments, experiences, or knowledge.

A text can therefore be read through a genre lens and also considered as fictional or nonfictional. For example, satire can appear in a novel, a sketch, a speech, a cartoon, or an essay. The point is not to classify texts into airtight categories, but to give students a flexible toolkit for interpretation.

### Dystopia

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To warn about possible futures by exaggerating current social, political, or technological problems.

#### Core conventions

Oppressive societies, surveillance, loss of freedom, propaganda, resistance, controlled language or behaviour.

#### Fictional example

George Orwell, *Nineteen Eighty-Four*

#### Nonfiction example

Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism*

### Satire

#### Most often seen as

Both

#### Core intent

To criticize people, institutions, or society by making them appear ridiculous, exaggerated, or absurd.

#### Core conventions

Irony, exaggeration, parody, humour, absurd situations, double meanings, indirect criticism.

#### Fictional example

Jonathan Swift, *Gulliver's Travels*

#### Nonfiction example

Jonathan Swift, "A Modest Proposal"

### Horror

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To create fear, unease, or disgust, often by confronting what society represses or fears.

#### Core conventions

Suspense, darkness, monsters, isolation, the unknown, bodily threat, supernatural or psychological disturbance.

#### Fictional example

Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein*

#### Nonfiction example

Truman Capote, *In Cold Blood*

### Crime / Detective

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To investigate wrongdoing and reveal hidden truth, guilt, or corruption.

#### Core conventions

Mystery, clues, suspects, investigation, false leads, tension, final revelation.

#### Fictional example

Arthur Conan Doyle, "The Adventure of the Speckled Band"

#### Nonfiction example

Sarah Koenig, *Serial*, season 1

### Romance

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To explore love, desire, intimacy, and the obstacles that prevent or enable relationships.

#### Core conventions

Attraction, emotional conflict, misunderstandings, social barriers, longing, confession, union or separation.

#### Fictional example

Jane Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*

#### Nonfiction example

Mandy Len Catron, "To Fall in Love With Anyone, Do This"

### Coming-of-age

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To show personal growth and the movement from childhood or innocence toward maturity.

#### Core conventions

Young protagonist, identity conflict, moral choices, rebellion, loss of innocence, learning through experience.

#### Fictional example

J. D. Salinger, *The Catcher in the Rye*

#### Nonfiction example

Maya Angelou, *I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings*

### Tragedy

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To explore suffering, downfall, loss, and the consequences of human flaws or social forces.

#### Core conventions

Serious conflict, fatal mistake or pressure, moral dilemma, reversal of fortune, emotional catharsis.

#### Fictional example

William Shakespeare, *Macbeth*

#### Nonfiction example

Asif Kapadia, *Amy*

### Comedy

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To entertain and reveal human weakness, conflict, or absurdity through humour.

#### Core conventions

Misunderstandings, comic timing, exaggeration, social embarrassment, reversals, happy or reconciliatory ending.

#### Fictional example

William Shakespeare, *A Midsummer Night's Dream*

#### Nonfiction example

Trevor Noah, *Son of Patricia*

### Science fiction

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To speculate about science, technology, society, and what it means to be human.

#### Core conventions

Future or alternative worlds, advanced technology, space, artificial intelligence, experiments, ethical dilemmas.

#### Fictional example

Ted Chiang, "Story of Your Life"

#### Nonfiction example

Yuval Noah Harari, *Homo Deus*

### Fantasy

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To imagine alternative worlds or magical possibilities in order to explore values, power, identity, and conflict.

#### Core conventions

Magic, invented worlds, quests, mythical beings, good and evil, transformation, symbolic objects.

#### Fictional example

J. R. R. Tolkien, *The Lord of the Rings*

#### Nonfiction example

Snorri Sturluson, *The Prose Edda*

### Adventure

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To entertain through risk, movement, challenge, and discovery.

#### Core conventions

Journey, danger, obstacles, exploration, survival, courage, fast-paced action.

#### Fictional example

Robert Louis Stevenson, *Treasure Island*

#### Nonfiction example

Jon Krakauer, *Into Thin Air*

### Opinion / Argument

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To persuade the audience to accept a viewpoint or take a position.

#### Core conventions

Clear claim, reasons, evidence, counterarguments, rhetorical appeals, direct engagement with the reader.

#### Fictional example

Harriet Beecher Stowe, *Uncle Tom's Cabin*

#### Nonfiction example

Martin Luther King Jr., "Letter from Birmingham Jail"

### Biography

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To present and interpret the life of a real person.

#### Core conventions

Chronology, key events, social context, achievements, conflicts, quotations, interpretation of character.

#### Fictional example

Hilary Mantel, *Wolf Hall*

#### Nonfiction example

Walter Isaacson, *Steve Jobs*

### Autobiography / Memoir

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To reflect on the writer's own life and give meaning to personal experience.

#### Core conventions

First-person narration, memory, reflection, selected life events, emotional honesty, personal voice.

#### Fictional example

Sue Townsend, *The Secret Diary of Adrian Mole, Aged 13¾*

#### Nonfiction example

Michelle Obama, *Becoming*

### Documentary

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To document, explain, investigate, or reveal aspects of reality.

#### Core conventions

Interviews, real footage, evidence, narration, expert voices, case studies, factual framing.

#### Fictional example

Rob Reiner, *This Is Spinal Tap*

#### Nonfiction example

Davis Guggenheim, *An Inconvenient Truth*

### News report

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To inform the audience about recent events.

#### Core conventions

Headline, lead, facts, quotations, who/what/where/when/why/how, neutral or concise style.

#### Fictional example

The Onion, "Area Man Passionate Defender Of What He Imagines Constitution To Be"

#### Nonfiction example

The New York Times, "Titanic Sinks Four Hours After Hitting Iceberg"

### Feature article

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To inform, explain, and engage readers by exploring a topic in depth.

#### Core conventions

Human-interest angle, descriptive opening, background, interviews, scenes, facts, narrative structure.

#### Fictional example

The Onion, "World's Scientists Admit They Just Don't Like Mice"

#### Nonfiction example

Gay Talese, "Frank Sinatra Has a Cold"

### Speech

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To persuade, inspire, commemorate, inform, or mobilize an audience.

#### Core conventions

Direct address, repetition, rhetorical questions, appeals to emotion and values, clear structure, memorable phrases.

#### Fictional example

President Whitmore's Independence Day speech in *Independence Day*

#### Nonfiction example

Martin Luther King Jr., "I Have a Dream"

### Essay

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To explore, reflect on, or argue about an idea.

#### Core conventions

Personal or intellectual voice, examples, structure, reflection, argument, open-ended thinking.

#### Fictional example

The appendix "The Principles of Newspeak" in George Orwell, *Nineteen Eighty-Four*

#### Nonfiction example

George Orwell, "Shooting an Elephant"

### Travel writing

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To describe places and journeys while reflecting on culture, identity, and encounter.

#### Core conventions

First-person perspective, setting descriptions, movement through places, cultural observations, reflection.

#### Fictional example

Jonathan Swift, *Gulliver's Travels*

#### Nonfiction example

Bill Bryson, *Notes from a Small Island*

### Gothic

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To create mystery, fear, and psychological unease, often by connecting place with hidden guilt or trauma.

#### Core conventions

Old houses or castles, secrets, madness, ghosts, doubles, darkness, forbidden knowledge, threatening atmosphere.

#### Fictional example

Charlotte Brontë, *Jane Eyre*

#### Nonfiction example

Albert and David Maysles, *Grey Gardens*

### Myth / Legend

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To explain origins, values, heroic ideals, or cultural beliefs through symbolic storytelling.

#### Core conventions

Gods or heroes, supernatural events, moral lessons, archetypes, quests, creation stories, oral tradition.

#### Fictional example

The myth of Prometheus

#### Nonfiction example

Snorri Sturluson, *The Prose Edda*

### Fable / Parable

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To teach a moral lesson through a simple, symbolic story.

#### Core conventions

Brief narrative, clear moral, animals or symbolic characters, contrast between wisdom and foolishness.

#### Fictional example

Aesop, "The Tortoise and the Hare"

#### Nonfiction example

The Parable of the Good Samaritan in the Gospel of Luke

### Propaganda

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To shape beliefs or behaviour, often by simplifying complex issues and appealing strongly to emotion.

#### Core conventions

Slogans, repetition, symbols, enemy images, selective facts, emotional appeals, us-versus-them thinking.

#### Fictional example

The "BIG BROTHER IS WATCHING YOU" posters in George Orwell, *Nineteen Eighty-Four*

#### Nonfiction example

James Montgomery Flagg, "I Want YOU for U.S. Army"

### Review

#### Most often seen as

Non-fictional

#### Core intent

To evaluate a work, product, performance, or experience and guide the audience's judgement.

#### Core conventions

Summary, evaluation, criteria, examples, praise and criticism, recommendation.

#### Fictional example

Anton Ego's restaurant review in *Ratatouille*

#### Nonfiction example

Roger Ebert, "Citizen Kane" review

### Elegy

#### Most often seen as

Both

#### Core intent

To mourn loss and reflect on memory, death, love, or meaning.

#### Core conventions

Serious tone, remembrance, grief, praise, reflection, contrast between past and present.

#### Fictional example

W. H. Auden, "Funeral Blues"

#### Nonfiction example

Martin Luther King Jr., "Eulogy for the Martyred Children"

### Lyric poetry / Song

#### Most often seen as

Both

#### Core intent

To express a concentrated feeling, mood, thought, or moment of experience.

#### Core conventions

First-person voice, imagery, rhythm, repetition, metaphor, emotional intensity, musical language.

#### Fictional example

T. S. Eliot, "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock"

#### Nonfiction example

Bob Dylan, "Blowin' in the Wind"

### Bildungsroman

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To trace a character's formation of identity over time, especially through education, conflict, and social experience.

#### Core conventions

Youth to adulthood, self-discovery, mentors, alienation, moral development, social integration or rejection.

#### Fictional example

Charles Dickens, *Great Expectations*

#### Nonfiction example

Tara Westover, *Educated*

### Historical fiction / Historical narrative

#### Most often seen as

Fictional

#### Core intent

To make the past meaningful by dramatizing historical events, conflicts, or experiences.

#### Core conventions

Period setting, historical details, real events or figures, invented characters, tension between fact and imagination.

#### Fictional example

Markus Zusak, *The Book Thief*

#### Nonfiction example

Ken Burns, *The Civil War*
