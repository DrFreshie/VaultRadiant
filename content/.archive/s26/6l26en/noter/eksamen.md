# Examiner's guide — Samlede spørgsmål 2

Use this as a quick support sheet for follow-up questions after the student's presentation. The points are not a model answer, but possible directions for the conversation.

## Question 1 — Emma Watson, “Speech on Gender Equality at the UN”
**Theme:** Voice  
**Focus:** Speaker's intention

### Short answer
Emma Watson argues that gender equality is not only a women's issue, but a human issue. She wants to redefine feminism as equality and invite men and boys to join the HeForShe campaign.

### Useful quotes
- “we need everyone involved” (ll. 2-3) — shows the inclusive purpose of the speech.
- “feminism by definition is the belief that men and women should have equal rights and opportunities” (ll. 11-12) — useful for discussing her definition of feminism.
- “Gender equality is your issue, too” (l. 31) — directly invites men into the campaign.
- “Both men and women should feel free to be sensitive. Both men and women should feel free to be strong” (ll. 42-43) — shows the rejection of gender stereotypes.
- “If not me, who? If not now, when?” (ll. 51-52, 59-60) — strong rhetorical call to action.

### Analytical points / follow-up questions
- **Purpose:** How does Watson try to persuade the audience to support HeForShe? (She combines personal experience, moral arguments, statistics, and a direct call to action.)
- **Audience:** Why does she address men directly? What effect does “formal invitation” have? (It makes men feel included and responsible instead of blamed or excluded.)
- **Ethos:** How does her role as UN Women Goodwill Ambassador help her credibility? (It gives her authority and shows that she speaks in an official international context.)
- **Pathos:** Which personal examples create emotional appeal? (Her examples from childhood, media sexualisation, girls dropping out of sport, and men's mental health.)
- **Logos:** How does she use definitions and statistics to support her argument? (She defines feminism clearly and uses numbers about pay equality, child marriage, and education.)
- **Language:** Notice repetition: “I think it is right…”, “If not me, who? If not now, when?” (The repetition makes the speech memorable and creates rhythm and urgency.)
- **Theme of voice:** How does Watson use her public voice to speak for others and create a movement? (She uses her celebrity platform to amplify a social issue and invite collective action.)
- **Gender stereotypes:** How does she show that stereotypes hurt both women and men? (Women are expected to be submissive, while men are pressured to be aggressive and hide emotions.)

## Question 2 — Jamaica Kincaid, “Girl”
**Theme:** Voice  
**Focus:** Message of the text

### Short answer
The text shows a mother giving her daughter instructions on how to behave as a proper woman. Its message is that girls are shaped and controlled by social expectations, especially around gender, respectability, sexuality, and domestic work.

### Useful quotes
- “Wash the white clothes on Monday…” (l. 1) — immediately establishes the commanding voice and domestic expectations.
- “is it true that you sing benna in Sunday school?” (ll. 6-7) — shows religious and cultural expectations.
- “walk like a lady and not like the slut you are so bent on becoming” (ll. 8-9) — central quote about gender, shame, and control.
- “don’t squat down to play marbles—you are not a boy” (l. 24) — clear example of gender roles.
- “but what if the baker won’t let me feel the bread?” (l. 34) — one of the girl's few interruptions, showing her limited voice.

### Analytical points / follow-up questions
- **Narrative voice:** Who speaks most of the time? What is the effect of the girl's voice appearing only briefly? (The mother dominates the text, showing how little space the girl has to define herself.)
- **Form:** The text is almost one long sentence. How does that affect the reading experience? (It feels overwhelming and breathless, like an endless stream of rules.)
- **Imperatives:** What do the many commands reveal about control and discipline? (They show that the girl is being trained and corrected constantly.)
- **Gender roles:** What kind of woman is the girl expected to become? (Respectable, domestic, obedient, sexually controlled, and socially acceptable.)
- **Fear and shame:** Why is the word “slut” repeated? What does it reveal about social pressure? (It shows fear of female sexuality and the threat of public shame.)
- **Culture and setting:** What details suggest Caribbean daily life and class expectations? (Foods, washing routines, Sunday school, benna, household work, and social manners.)
- **Mother-daughter relationship:** Is the mother caring, oppressive, or both? (Both: she passes on practical knowledge, but also controls and shames the girl.)
- **Theme of voice:** Does the girl have a voice? How is her voice limited? (She only speaks twice and is immediately overruled, so her voice is marginalised.)

## Question 3 — Leonora Carrington, “The Debutante”
**Theme:** Belonging  
**Focus:** Theme of the text

### Short answer
A young debutante hates high society and feels more connected to animals than to people her own age. She lets a hyena replace her at a ball, leading to violence and chaos. The story criticises social expectations, class rituals, and the pressure to fit in.

### Useful quotes
- “I was better acquainted with animals than with the young girls of my age” (ll. 1-3) — shows the narrator's alienation from her social group.
- “It was to escape from the world that I found myself each day at the zoo” (ll. 3-4) — connects the zoo to escape and belonging.
- “I had always detested balls” (ll. 7-8) — shows her rejection of upper-class rituals.
- “You have only to go in my place” (l. 17) — key moment where the hyena becomes her double.
- “I will wear her face this evening in place of my own” (ll. 38-39) — useful for discussing masks, identity, and performance.
- “she removed her face and ate it” (ll. 68-69) — the shocking ending breaks the social performance completely.

### Analytical points / follow-up questions
- **Belonging:** Where does the narrator feel she belongs — with humans, family, society, or animals? (She feels more at home with the hyena than with her family or upper-class society.)
- **Symbolism:** What might the hyena represent? Freedom, rebellion, instinct, or the narrator's hidden self? (The hyena can represent her wild, rebellious side that refuses polite society.)
- **Surrealism:** How does the bizarre plot challenge realism and social norms? (It uses absurd violence and fantasy to expose how strange social rituals can be.)
- **Class and gender:** What does the debutante ball represent? (It represents upper-class expectations, femininity, marriageability, and social performance.)
- **Mother:** How does the mother represent social control and respectability? (She arranges the ball and expects her daughter to behave properly and fit in.)
- **Violence and comedy:** Why is the murder of the maid described so casually? What effect does this create? (The casual tone creates dark humour and makes the story disturbing and surreal.)
- **Masks and identity:** What is the significance of the hyena wearing the maid's face? (It suggests that identity in society is a mask or performance.)
- **Theme question:** Is the narrator escaping society, or revealing that society itself is animalistic? (Both readings work: she escapes social pressure, but the story also shows society as cruel and artificial.)

## Question 4 — Soledad Castillo, “At 14, I walked through the desert to reach the US”
**Theme:** Borders  
**Focus:** Writer's intention

### Short answer
Castillo tells her personal migration story to challenge negative stereotypes about migrants. She wants readers to understand that crossing the border is not the end of migrants' struggles, and that young migrants need protection, support, and humane policies.

### Useful quotes
- “we were the poorest family in our small neighborhood” (ll. 2-3) — establishes poverty as part of the reason for migration.
- “I was only 12” (l. 12) — emphasises her vulnerability and childhood trauma.
- “I was 14” (l. 20) — repeated age marker that stresses how young she was during the journey.
- “reaching the US wasn’t the end of my struggles, only the beginning” (ll. 23-24) — central statement of the article's message.
- “Many Americans think that migrants come here to take their jobs… These ideas are not right” (ll. 35-36) — directly challenges stereotypes.
- “Migrant narratives don’t end once we arrive in our new communities” (ll. 54-55) — key concluding message.

### Analytical points / follow-up questions
- **Purpose:** What does Castillo want the reader to think or do after reading? (She wants readers to listen to migrants, reject stereotypes, and support humane policies and practical help.)
- **Personal testimony:** How does the first-person narrative affect credibility and emotion? (It makes the story feel authentic, personal, and emotionally powerful.)
- **Stereotypes:** How does she respond to the idea that migrants come to “take jobs” or exploit the country? (She says migrants come to survive, work hard, help family, and contribute.)
- **Structure:** How does the text move from trauma to survival to activism? (It begins with abuse and migration, then describes hardship in the US, and ends with helping others.)
- **Borders:** Are the borders only geographical, or also social, economic, and legal? (They are also barriers of poverty, language, housing, education, and legal status.)
- **Contrast:** How does she contrast the dream of the US with the reality after arrival? (The US seems full of opportunity, but she faces work, abuse, depression, and poverty.)
- **Pathos:** Which moments are especially emotional, and why? (The abuse, the journey, child labour, and her isolation are emotional because she is so young and vulnerable.)
- **Ending:** Why does she ask readers to listen to migrant stories and support migrants? (She wants to humanise migrants and show that their stories continue after arrival.)

## Question 5 — Sandy Rivera, “Undocumented, Yet Hopeful”
**Theme:** Borders  
**Focus:** Rhetorical strategies

### Short answer
Rivera uses her own story as an undocumented immigrant to argue for dignity, solidarity, and immigration reform. She presents herself as undocumented but unafraid, and encourages others to keep fighting for justice.

### Useful quotes
- “I am a sister, I am a daughter, I am a friend, I am a student” (ll. 3-5) — builds ethos by showing she is more than her legal status.
- “I am undocumented and unafraid” (l. 5) — powerful statement of identity and courage.
- “I chose to hide in the shadows” (ll. 17-18) — shows fear and invisibility before empowerment.
- “Immigrant parents are the original Dreamers” (l. 29) — memorable rhetorical claim and repetition.
- “We must continue to fight” (ll. 44-46) — creates urgency and collective action.
- “undocumented, unafraid, and unapologetic” (l. 47) — strong triad showing pride and resistance.

### Analytical points / follow-up questions
- **Ethos:** How does Rivera present her identity: student, daughter, Latina, undocumented? (She presents herself as a complex, credible person and not just a legal status.)
- **Pathos:** How does she create sympathy through fear, shame, hope, and family sacrifice? (She describes hiding her identity, her parents' sacrifices, and her hope for the future.)
- **Repetition:** Discuss “Immigrant parents are the original Dreamers” and “We must continue to fight…” (Repetition strengthens her message and turns it into a shared political call.)
- **Contrast:** How does she contrast fear and shame with pride and courage? (She moves from hiding and assimilation to being “undocumented, unafraid, and unapologetic.”)
- **Inclusive language:** What is the effect of “we”, “our”, and “brothers and sisters”? (It builds solidarity and makes the audience feel part of a collective struggle.)
- **Code-switching:** Why open with “Good afternoon. Buenas tardes.”? (It signals bilingual identity and addresses both English- and Spanish-speaking audiences.)
- **Political context:** What role does DACA play in the speech? (DACA gives her access to dreams and opportunities, and its removal creates urgency.)
- **Theme of borders:** How are legal status, belonging, and identity connected? (Her undocumented status creates barriers, but she still claims belonging and identity in the US.)

## Question 6 — Sophie Gonzales, *Perfect on Paper*
**Theme:** Belonging  
**Focus:** Characterisation of Darcy

### Short answer
Darcy is a bisexual high school student who fears she will no longer belong in the queer community if she has feelings for a boy. Through the support of the Q&Q Club, she learns that her identity is valid regardless of who she dates.

### Useful quotes
- “I’m bi” (ll. 11-12) — direct statement of Darcy's identity.
- “I feel like if I’m with a guy, I won’t belong here properly anymore” (ll. 18-19) — central conflict about belonging.
- “What she’s describing is internalized biphobia” (ll. 27-28) — names and explains the main issue.
- “Like being with a guy magically makes me straight” (ll. 41-42) — shows Darcy's frustration with stereotypes about bisexuality.
- “You’re queer” (ll. 55-60) — repeated affirmation from the community.
- “We just belonged because we belonged” (l. 75) — key final statement about unconditional belonging.

### Analytical points / follow-up questions
- **Characterisation:** How is Darcy shown as anxious, self-aware, honest, and vulnerable? (Her first-person thoughts show nervousness, but she still chooses to speak openly.)
- **Conflict:** What is Darcy's internal conflict? What is her external conflict with the group/community? (Internally she doubts her queer identity; externally she fears judgement from queer people.)
- **Internalized biphobia:** How does the text explain this concept? (It is when bisexual people absorb society's negative ideas and start doubting themselves.)
- **Belonging:** Why does Darcy fear being excluded from queer spaces? (She worries that dating a boy will make others see her as not queer enough.)
- **Dialogue:** How does the club's repeated “You're queer” function emotionally? (It reassures her and turns acceptance into a powerful collective statement.)
- **Community:** What kind of community does the Q&Q Club represent? (It represents support, recognition, and a safe space for identity.)
- **Narration:** How does first-person narration help us understand Darcy's fear? (It gives direct access to her thoughts, shame, and relief.)
- **Ending:** What is meant by “We just belonged because we belonged”? (Belonging does not need proof; the community accepts identity as valid.)

## Question 7 — Sherman Alexie, “Superman and Me”
**Theme:** Voice  
**Focus:** Theme of the text

### Short answer
Alexie describes how he learned to read through a Superman comic and how reading gave him power, identity, and a voice. The essay shows literacy as a way to resist stereotypes and “save lives,” especially for Native American children on reservations.

### Useful quotes
- “I learned to read with a Superman comic book” (l. 1) — simple opening that introduces literacy and popular culture.
- “Our house was filled with books” (ll. 13-14) — shows the father's influence and reading environment.
- “a paragraph was a fence that held words” (ll. 21-22) — important metaphor for how he understands language and the world.
- “Superman is breaking down the door” (ll. 35-38) — central image of breaking barriers.
- “he might have been called a prodigy… [but] is simply an oddity” (ll. 42-44) — exposes racial stereotypes and low expectations.
- “I am trying to save our lives” (ll. 61-62) — powerful ending about literacy, survival, and voice.

### Analytical points / follow-up questions
- **Theme of voice:** How does reading help Alexie find his own voice as a writer? (Reading gives him language, confidence, and a way to tell Native stories.)
- **Superman metaphor:** What does “breaking down the door” symbolise? (It symbolises breaking through barriers such as poverty, racism, and low expectations.)
- **Paragraph metaphor:** How does Alexie use paragraphs to understand the world? (He sees people, places, and communities as connected units with meaning.)
- **Identity and stereotypes:** Why would a gifted Indian boy be seen as an “oddity” instead of a prodigy? (Because racist stereotypes make people expect less from Native children.)
- **Father's influence:** How does his father's love of books shape him? (He loves his father and copies his love of reading.)
- **Tone:** Where is the tone proud, painful, angry, or hopeful? (He is proud of reading, hurt by stereotypes, angry at limitations, and hopeful when teaching children.)
- **Purpose:** Why does Alexie visit reservation schools? (He wants to inspire Native students and show them that writing is possible for them.)
- **Ending:** What does “I am trying to save our lives” mean? (Literacy and storytelling can help young people survive, resist, and imagine futures.)
